\section{Introduction}

Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) are increasingly used for road monitoring, traffic understanding, emergency response, and perimeter observation. Vehicle detection in such imagery is difficult because targets are often small, the viewing geometry changes rapidly, backgrounds are cluttered, and illumination can vary substantially within a flight. A lightweight detector is therefore attractive, but lightweight processing must not come at the cost of an evaluation protocol that overstates performance. In particular, sequential or spatially adjacent acquisitions can create visually similar samples, so an apparently strong validation result may partly reflect overlap between training and validation content rather than robust generalization.

RGB cameras remain the most common sensing modality because they provide color, texture, and spatial detail. Their usefulness can decrease under low light, shadows, glare, or adverse contrast. Thermal sensing can preserve heat-related contrast when visible appearance becomes less informative, while event sensing provides sparse temporal changes with high dynamic range. These modalities are complementary, but complementarity alone does not guarantee that a detector will use them robustly. A fusion module can over-rely on one stream; furthermore, one stream can be absent or degraded at deployment time. The practical question addressed here is therefore not only whether RGB, thermal, and event inputs can be combined, but whether a compact detector can combine them under a protocol that makes the resulting evidence interpretable.

This paper develops RA-RepDet, a reliability-aware RGB--thermal--event detector built around a RepViT-M0.9 backbone \cite{RepViT2024}, a feature pyramid network (FPN) \cite{FPN2017}, and an FCOS anchor-free detection head \cite{FCOS2019}. The model operates on five-channel samples composed of three RGB channels, one thermal channel, and one event channel. The matched early-fusion baseline, denoted R0, uses a learned $5\!\rightarrow\!3$ input projection before the shared RepViT--FPN--FCOS stack. The reliability-aware variants use modality-specific stems and a learned softmax gate that assigns sample-dependent weights to RGB, thermal, and event features before projection into the backbone. To reduce reliance on a single stream, modality dropout is applied during training; the R4 configuration uses dropout probability $p=0.20$.

A second contribution is methodological. The original random split was audited for exact RGB-content overlap and contained 153 validation samples with an exact RGB-content match in training, corresponding to 0.072927 of validation images. This observation does not imply full five-channel byte duplication, but it is sufficient to make the random split unsuitable for headline evidence. We therefore report the main results on a frozen block64/guard16 split containing 7439 training images, 2213 validation images, and 837 guard images. The resulting training and validation partitions have zero exact RGB-content matches and zero same-family guard-band violations. The protocol is motivated by the broader recognition that split leakage and adaptive model selection can produce optimistic machine-learning conclusions \cite{Kapoor2023Leakage,Cawley2010Overfitting}.

The evidence is intentionally scoped. The controlled clean-split comparison uses seeds 0 and 2 and is interpreted as controlled replication rather than a statistical-significance analysis. The missing-modality study uses synthetic removal by zeroing one modality at evaluation time; it is therefore a stress evaluation, not a complete representation of real sensor failure. Finally, an RGB-only YOLO11n result is included as an external reference, not as a matched architecture-only ablation. Within these limits, the contributions of this study are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We provide a lightweight RepViT--FPN--FCOS tri-modal UAV vehicle detector and a matched early-fusion baseline operating on the same five-channel representation.
    \item We introduce reliability-aware stem fusion with modality-dropout training and evaluate the R0, R1, R2, and R4 variants under a leakage-aware blocked validation protocol.
    \item We quantify synthetic single-modality removal behavior and report reliability-gate observations, while separating measured behavior from causal claims about physical modality importance.
    \item We document split integrity, frozen table provenance, and the scope of the RGB-only external comparison so that the manuscript's claims remain aligned with the available evidence.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{UAV Vehicle Detection}

UAV imagery poses a demanding version of object detection because object scale, viewpoint, density, blur, and background appearance can change within and across scenes. Benchmarks such as VisDrone and UAVDT capture several of these difficulties and have encouraged methods that retain sensitivity to small and crowded targets \cite{VisDrone2018,UAVDT2018}. More broadly, modern object detection has developed through two-stage region-proposal approaches, dense one-stage detectors, and focal-loss-based treatment of class imbalance \cite{FasterRCNN2015,RetinaNet2017}. FCOS is an influential anchor-free one-stage formulation that predicts object locations directly rather than relying on hand-designed anchor boxes \cite{FCOS2019}.

The present work adopts an FCOS head because the research goal is not to introduce a new detection head but to study whether a compact tri-modal front end can feed a lightweight detector effectively. RepViT provides a mobile-oriented backbone family with an efficient convolutional design \cite{RepViT2024}. Combined with an FPN \cite{FPN2017}, this design provides a practical shared architecture for a matched comparison between simple early fusion and reliability-aware fusion. This comparison is important because changing both the architecture and the modality configuration at once would prevent attribution of an observed performance difference to the fusion design.

\subsection{Visible, Thermal, and Event Perception}

Visible and thermal sensing have been combined in many perception settings because their strengths differ across illumination and heat-contrast conditions. Resources such as KAIST multispectral pedestrian detection, FLIR ADAS, LLVIP, and DroneVehicle illustrate the value and challenges of visible--infrared learning \cite{KAIST2015,FLIRADAS,LLVIP2021,DroneVehicle2021}. These settings also show that a thermal stream is not uniformly informative: its contribution depends on scene contrast, calibration, target appearance, and the condition of the visible sensor.

Event cameras provide another source of complementary information. By recording brightness changes asynchronously, event streams can preserve temporal cues and high dynamic range under conditions in which conventional frames are less stable. Event-based vision surveys and datasets including MVSEC, DSEC, and GEN1 have established a broad foundation for event-driven perception \cite{Gallego2020EventSurvey,MVSEC2018,DSEC2021,Gen1Events}. In this paper, events are represented as one channel in an already constructed five-channel sample. The work therefore evaluates fusion of the available event representation; it does not make claims about dynamic event-window selection, asynchronous stream reconstruction, or raw event-stream processing.

Cross-modal supervision and modality hallucination have shown that information from one sensor can regularize another during learning \cite{CrossModalDistillation2016,ModalityHallucination2016}. RA-RepDet follows the related but narrower idea that a detector should be able to change its feature mixture when the available streams differ. The method does not attempt to reconstruct a missing modality. Instead, it learns a reliability-weighted feature combination and uses modality-dropout training to reduce dependence on any one input stream.

\subsection{Missing-Modality Robustness and Reliability-Aware Fusion}

Missing-modality learning studies the case in which one or more sources are unavailable, corrupted, or intentionally withheld. Modality dropout and hetero-modal learning are established approaches for reducing brittle reliance on a single channel \cite{ModDrop2016,HeMIS2016}. Such methods are especially relevant to multi-sensor UAV platforms, where sensor streams can differ in quality or may be temporarily unavailable. However, robustness terminology requires care. A zeroed-channel evaluation is useful for controlled comparison, but it cannot reproduce all real effects of thermal drift, optical blur, temporal misalignment, sensor saturation, or field-of-view mismatch.

The reliability mechanism in this study is intentionally simple. It predicts three weights from pooled modality-stem features and uses a softmax normalization so that the fused representation is a convex combination of stem outputs. The purpose is to make fusion adaptive at the representation level while retaining a lightweight backbone-compatible interface. The reported gate values are inspected as observations of the learned computation. They should not be interpreted as a calibrated probability that a physical sensor is reliable, nor as proof that one modality causes a particular performance outcome.

\subsection{Leakage-Aware Evaluation}

Evaluation integrity is central when a dataset contains sequential, nearby, or repeated visual content. Exact duplicates, near duplicates, shared scene families, and adaptive use of validation feedback can all lead to overly favorable conclusions if not recognized. The need to consider dataset quality, overlap, and evaluation leakage has been emphasized in work on generalization and machine-learning practice \cite{Recht2019ImageNet,Northcutt2021LabelErrors,Kapoor2023Leakage}. In the present setting, the relevant audit is deliberately modest: exact RGB-content matches are used as a detectable signal of visual overlap. The audit does not claim that RGB equality captures every form of dependence. Instead, it motivates a blocked split and guard band that provide a stricter basis for the manuscript's headline validation evidence.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Problem Definition and Overall Architecture}

Each local TriAir sample is represented as a five-channel tensor
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x} = [\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{rgb}},\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{th}},\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ev}}],
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{rgb}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times H\times W}$, $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{th}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times H\times W}$, and $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ev}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times H\times W}$. The detection task is single-class vehicle detection. At training time, TriAir class 0 is converted to torchvision label 1, while background remains label 0. The shared detector uses RepViT-M0.9 feature stages with channel sizes 48, 96, 192, and 384. An FPN maps the stage features to 128 channels, and FCOS performs dense object classification and box regression \cite{TorchvisionFCOS}.

\subsection{Matched Early-Fusion Baseline}

The R0 baseline is designed to expose all five channels to the detector while retaining a standard three-channel backbone input. A $1\times1$ convolution projects the five-channel tensor to three channels, after which the projected representation is processed by the RepViT backbone, FPN, and FCOS head. This approach is a conventional early-fusion design: cross-modal interactions are learned after the shared projection, and no explicit modality-specific reliability weighting is imposed at the input.

R0 is the matched baseline for the reliability variants because the backbone family, FPN width, detection head, training split, image size, and evaluation code are held fixed. Consequently, R0 versus R1, R2, and R4 is the primary comparison for assessing the fusion front end. The RGB-only YOLO11n result reported later serves a different role. Because it uses a different input configuration and detector implementation, it provides practical context but does not isolate an architecture-only effect.

\subsection{Reliability-Aware Tri-Modal Fusion}

The reliability-aware variants first process the three modality groups separately. RGB is passed through a Conv2d$(3,16,3,\text{padding}=1)$--batch-normalization--SiLU stem. Thermal and event inputs each use a corresponding Conv2d$(1,16,3,\text{padding}=1)$--batch-normalization--SiLU stem. Let the stem outputs be $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{rgb}}$, $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{th}}$, and $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ev}}$. Global average pooling produces a 16-dimensional descriptor for each stem, and concatenating the descriptors yields a 48-dimensional vector. A two-layer reliability estimator maps this vector through Linear$(48,16)$, SiLU, and Linear$(16,3)$.

The reliability vector is normalized as
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \mathrm{softmax}\left(g\left([\mathrm{GAP}(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{rgb}});\mathrm{GAP}(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{th}});\mathrm{GAP}(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ev}})]\right)\right),
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\alpha}=[\alpha_{\mathrm{rgb}},\alpha_{\mathrm{th}},\alpha_{\mathrm{ev}}]$ and the elements sum to one. The fused feature is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{fuse}}=\alpha_{\mathrm{rgb}}\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{rgb}}+\alpha_{\mathrm{th}}\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{th}}+\alpha_{\mathrm{ev}}\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ev}}.
\end{equation}
A projection maps the 16-channel fused tensor to the three-channel interface used by RepViT. The softmax gate is a learned feature-selection mechanism. It is not calibrated as a probability of sensor health, and the values are reported only as an audit of the model's internal weighting behavior.

\subsection{Modality-Dropout Training}

R1 uses reliability-aware fusion without modality dropout. R2 uses modality dropout with $p=0.15$, and R4 uses $p=0.20$. During training, modality dropout can zero selected sensor streams so that the detector encounters incomplete combinations rather than only the full RGB--thermal--event input. At ordinary full-modality inference, all available streams are provided. The method is therefore not a separate detector for each missingness pattern; it is one shared detector trained to reduce brittle reliance on a single stream.

For controlled robustness evaluation, RGB, thermal, or event channels are zeroed one at a time. This procedure is useful because it gives the same explicit intervention to each variant. It should nevertheless be interpreted as synthetic missingness. It does not model the full physical behavior of sensor outages, geometric misregistration, changing event integration windows, or dynamic thermal contrast.

\section{Dataset and Leakage-Aware Evaluation Protocol}

\subsection{TriAir Representation and Labels}

The local TriAir representation contains 10489 five-channel samples. Each sample combines RGB, thermal, and event information, and labels are stored as YOLO-format vehicle boxes. There are 9751 images with label text files, 738 images without label text files, and one empty label text file. Rather than discarding missing or empty label files, the data pipeline treats them as empty-target images. This treatment preserves the sample inventory and avoids introducing an undocumented selection step. Across the local representation, 30634 valid vehicle boxes are available.

The paper reports results on this locally represented five-channel dataset and does not assert redistribution rights, licence terms, or a public release policy. Those governance details remain author-confirmed submission items. The manuscript therefore confines its data description to the repository-documented representation, label handling, and evaluation protocol.

\subsection{RGB-Content Duplicate Audit}

Before selecting the headline split, the project audited the original random split for exact RGB-content matches between training and validation samples. The audit found 153 validation samples with at least one exact RGB-content match in training. These samples account for 0.072927 of the validation partition. The test is intentionally limited to exact RGB content. It does not establish full five-channel byte duplication, nor does it capture every possible dependency induced by nearby frames or related scenes. Its value is diagnostic: it identifies a concrete form of visual overlap that should not remain hidden when headline validation results are interpreted.

The audit result motivated the use of a blocked split with a guard band. \subsection{Controlled Clean-Split Ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:controlled-clean-split-ablation} reports the matched tri-modal ablation. R0 early fusion achieved mean AP50$=0.938136$ across the two controlled seeds. Adding reliability-aware fusion without dropout in R1 increased mean AP50 to 0.953245. R2 with $p=0.15$ achieved mean AP50$=0.959656$, while R4 with $p=0.20$ achieved the highest mean AP50, 0.962495. R4 also obtained mean $F1@0.50=0.920861$ and mean AP75$=0.891266$.

The per-seed AP50 values for R4 were 0.965012 and 0.959977. These values exceed the corresponding R0 AP50 values of 0.938560 and 0.937711 for the same seed set. R4 is therefore the selected main variant for the clean-split controlled comparison. The result supports the usefulness of the reliability-aware front end and modality-dropout configuration within this architecture and protocol. It does not establish that the same ordering will hold for every backbone, dataset, sensor suite, or unseen deployment condition.

The AP75 outcome is more nuanced. R4 achieved mean AP75$=0.891266$, whereas R2 achieved mean AP75$=0.884759$. At the seed level, R2 had the larger AP75 in seed 0, while R4 had the larger AP75 in seed 2. This pattern reinforces the reason for reporting both per-seed rows and the two-seed aggregate. The paper consequently treats R4 selection as a controlled design decision supported by the overall recorded evidence, rather than as an unconditional claim about every detection-quality metric under every run.

\subsection{Robustness to Synthetic Missing Modalities}

The missing-modality evaluation measures how the trained models respond when one input channel group is zeroed. R1, which uses reliability fusion without modality dropout, is fragile under this intervention. Its mean AP50 is 0.712989 without RGB, 0.330777 without thermal, and 0.568720 without event. R2 improves the three conditions to 0.907911, 0.670260, and 0.958349, respectively. R4 further improves the corresponding means to 0.916051 without RGB, 0.718277 without thermal, and 0.961577 without event.

These results are consistent with the intended role of modality dropout: exposure to incomplete inputs during training is associated with substantially better synthetic missing-stream behavior than reliability fusion alone. The strongest residual performance occurs when the event stream is removed, while thermal removal remains the hardest condition. Even for R4, the mean AP50 under synthetic no-thermal evaluation is 0.718277, well below its full-modality mean AP50. The paper treats this degradation as a limitation rather than suppressing it in favor of the stronger no-event outcome.

The experiment does not establish that the thermal channel is universally the most important physical sensor. Zeroing one channel changes the learned representation in a controlled computational way, but real deployment failures can involve different noise processes and cross-sensor relationships. The result should therefore be read as a model-specific stress evaluation of the available five-channel representation. Figure~\ref{fig:fig-4-missing-modality-robustness} will display the complete per-seed condition set, including the thermal-removal weakness.

\section{Discussion}

The controlled ablation supports three bounded conclusions. First, the reliability-aware fusion variants improve the matched R0 early-fusion baseline under the frozen leakage-aware split. R4 provides the strongest mean AP50 among the recorded R0, R1, R2, and R4 variants. Second, modality-dropout training is associated with substantially improved synthetic missing-modality behavior relative to R1 without dropout. Third, the clean-split protocol changes the interpretation of the paper from one based on a random partition with documented exact RGB overlap to one based on a split with explicit blocked separation and guard-band exclusions.

The main result is not simply that a larger or more complex model achieves a higher number. R0 and R4 differ primarily in the fusion front end and training-time modality-dropout configuration while using the same broad detector family, input size, split, and evaluation code. This structure makes the R0--R4 contrast more informative than a comparison across unrelated detectors. Nevertheless, R4's higher AP50 should be understood as an outcome for this configuration on this local TriAir representation. The evidence does not establish universal superiority of reliability-aware fusion across all UAV datasets or tri-modal acquisition systems.

The missing-modality study also deserves a careful reading. Its most positive result is that R4 retains AP50 above 0.9 under synthetic removal of RGB and close to its full-input result under synthetic removal of the event channel. Its least favorable result is the no-thermal condition, where AP50 falls to 0.718277. This contrast indicates that the learned detector is not equally robust to all interventions. It also argues against presenting reliability-aware fusion as a complete solution to sensor degradation. A more comprehensive robustness study would need realistic sensor corruptions, spatial and temporal alignment perturbations, and possibly raw event-stream controls.

The reliability-weight audit gives supporting evidence for adaptive fusion but does not by itself explain all detection outcomes. Under thermal removal, the gate shifts substantial mean weight toward RGB, but the thermal coefficient is not identically zero. The softmax formulation enforces a relative allocation over three stem features; a nonzero coefficient on a zeroed or degraded input does not necessarily imply that the detector treats that modality as physically useful. Likewise, a high RGB coefficient does not prove that RGB is causally responsible for a given detection. The appropriate interpretation is narrower: the gate changes systematically across the evaluated synthetic conditions.

The RGB-only YOLO11n result provides useful application context, because its mean AP50 and AP75 are lower than those of the R4 tri-modal detector under the same clean split. However, the contrast combines a change in modality availability with a change in implementation. It should therefore remain an external baseline rather than being used to claim that the reliability module alone explains the gap. This distinction is important for an honest manuscript: a strong practical comparison can coexist with a limited causal interpretation.

Finally, the split audit is part of the research contribution rather than only a preprocessing detail. A random split with documented exact RGB-content overlap may still be useful for exploratory development, but it should not carry the headline claim. By moving to a frozen blocked split with a guard band, the work prioritizes a more conservative and auditable validation statement. This choice may make the reported narrative less convenient than simply selecting the best number from a random partition, but it strengthens the connection between the reported metrics and the evidence they are intended to support.

\section{Limitations}

This study has several limitations. First, the controlled replication uses two seeds. The paired runs provide useful consistency checks, but they do not constitute a statistical-significance analysis. Second, all main experiments use one locally represented dataset. The results therefore do not establish cross-dataset generalization. Third, the missing-modality experiments use zeroed channels as synthetic interventions. They do not represent the full range of real thermal, RGB, or event-sensor degradation, including temporal asynchrony, geometric misalignment, blur, saturation, or changing event accumulation windows.

Fourth, thermal removal is the hardest synthetic missingness condition for R4, with mean AP50$=0.718277$. This residual weakness is a substantive result and indicates room for future work on more explicit robustness, uncertainty, and alignment mechanisms. Fifth, the current reliability weights are descriptive outputs of a learned softmax gate. They are not externally calibrated reliability scores and should not be interpreted as causal modality-importance measurements.

Sixth, the manuscript remains a first draft with author information, declarations, data-governance details, release/archive information, and environment records intentionally pending. Tables~1--7 are evidence-locked, but Figures~1--6 require author-approved final assets before a formal submission package can be assembled. In particular, the qualitative panel figure requires author-controlled selection and composition. Finally, the conclusions use validation-only wording. A stronger independent held-out claim would require a separately frozen evaluation protocol and additional approved evidence.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presents RA-RepDet, a lightweight reliability-aware RGB--thermal--event vehicle detector for UAV imagery. The method combines modality-specific stems, softmax-weighted fusion, a RepViT-M0.9 backbone, an FPN, an FCOS head, and training-time modality dropout. The evaluation is conducted on a frozen leakage-aware block64/guard16 split with 7439 training images, 2213 validation images, and 837 guard images, with zero exact RGB train/validation matches and zero same-family guard-band violations.

Across controlled seeds 0 and 2, the R4 configuration with modality dropout $p=0.20$ achieved mean AP50$=0.962495$, AP75$=0.891266$, and $F1@0.50=0.920861$. Relative to the matched R0 early-fusion baseline, the evidence supports the use of reliability-aware fusion and modality-dropout training in this setting. R4 also improves synthetic missing-modality performance relative to the no-dropout reliability variant, while thermal removal remains a clear limitation. The manuscript further separates the matched tri-modal ablation from the RGB-only YOLO11n external baseline and preserves the distinction between observed gate behavior and causal sensor-importance claims.

The resulting first draft provides an evidence-locked basis for author review. Future steps are limited to author-confirmed metadata and declarations, final figure approval, data-governance and release decisions, environment documentation, strict preflight closure, and final compile review; none of those pending steps change the experimental evidence reported here.
